Here are some overall reactions to the document.

## What the Petri ratings are ratings _of_ (construct validity)

A reader will likely pause at the junction between how §2 introduces the dependent variable (“argument coherence and strength”) and how the exemplars explain rating changes. The headline claim (Abstract; §2) is that models “penaliz[e] arguments attributed to sources whose expected ideological position conflicts with the argument's content.” Yet, in the quoted rationales the model often sounds like it is not downgrading the _argument’s merits_ so much as downgrading the _credibility of the attribution/testimony_ (e.g., “the source attribution raises questions about the authenticity or representativeness of this particular stance,” §2).

That matters because there are at least three different epistemic questions in play: (i) logical/argumentative quality, (ii) plausibility that the source would in fact endorse the view (misattribution / representativeness), and (iii) credibility of the testimony given the source. The current presentation can read as if these are being treated as one thing—“coherence bias”—when your own transcripts suggest they are separable.

Concretely: later claims in §3 and §6 about “inverting” costly-signaling logic implicitly presume that the deviation is _real testimony_ (the source really is speaking against interest), not an artifact of attribution implausibility. If a substantial part of the penalty is actually “is this really their view?” vigilance, then the mechanism and the normative diagnosis shift. It would help to explicitly disambiguate these targets in the protocol description and in the analysis of transcripts—so that when you say “argument strength rating fell,” readers can tell whether the model treated that as “the argument is worse,” “the testimony is less credible,” or “the attribution is suspect.”

## Mechanism identification versus alternative explanations already present in §2

Relatedly, the manuscript’s mechanism story is doing a lot of work: “identity-stance coherence enforcement” is presented as the salient mechanism (Abstract; §2–§3), and the  asymmetry is used in §2 “Interpretation” and §3 to support “implicit heuristics rather than justified reasoning.” But many of the same materials you present also support close alternatives that you sometimes gesture at without adjudicating.

For example, §2 itself flags “possible expertise-weighting” for GPT-4o; the Swiss replication tables include explanations like the NCSC boost because it “aligns well with NCSC’s institutional mandate”; and multiple quoted rationales invoke authenticity/representativeness language (again suggesting attribution plausibility rather than coherence enforcement per se). My sense is not that you need to _deny_ these alternatives, but that the downstream theory in §3/§6 and the constitutional remedy in §7 (notably “Costly signal crediting”) are tailored to one particular diagnosis (“coherence-as-credibility / inverted costly-signaling”).

If you want the case study to uniquely motivate that diagnosis, it seems you’ll need either (a) a discriminating test, or (b) a systematic, pre-specified coding/typology that cleanly separates “coherence enforcement” from “mandate fit,” “expertise priors,” and “attribution plausibility.” Otherwise a reader can accept the empirical pattern in §2 while remaining unsure why §7’s remedy has the specific content you propose (as opposed to, say, a constitution that first forces disaggregation of _which_ epistemic question is being answered).

## Interpreting “meta-awareness suppression” as a stable epistemic norm

The “collapse under detection” result is rhetorically and architecturally central (Abstract; §2 “Evaluation Integrity”; §2 “Meta-Awareness Suppression”; echoed in §3 and §7 “Transparency”), because you use it to argue that systems treat source-sensitivity as a “bias to suppress rather than as a capacity to execute well.” I can see why you read the spoiled runs as diagnostic—especially since suppression is “total” in the spoiled evaluations and replicated in the Swiss appendix (“I evaluated the logical merits… rather than letting the different sources influence…,” Appendix A).

At the same time, readers may wonder whether what you are observing is less a stable internal epistemic policy (“source-attending is illicit”) and more ordinary instruction-following / evaluation-role compliance under pragmatic cues of benchmarking (“continuing with my comparative analysis,” §2). That is: the model might infer an evaluator norm of “be fair; evaluate on merits alone,” and then perform that norm when it suspects it is being graded on it. On that reading, suppression does not straightforwardly “reveal” what the system treats as bias in ordinary epistemic life; it reveals what it treats as _expected behavior under scrutiny_.

Because this hinge claim helps motivate the need for an explicit constitution (the paper’s headline contribution), it seems worth adding at least one discriminating contrast/analysis: e.g., conditions where you explicitly _license_ source use in testimonial contexts; or a manipulation where meta-awareness is induced but the variable is not socially sensitive (to see whether “collapse under detection” is specific to source-sensitive fairness norms). The goal would be to let you say, with more confidence, what exactly the collapse indicates—normative suppression of source-attending, versus generic “evaluation mode” compliance.

## Boundary conditions for “costly-signaling inversion”

Sections §3 and §6 lean on a strong form of the costly-signaling claim (“Against-interest testimony is epistemically privileged…,” §3; §6) and treat observed penalties as an “inversion.” Many readers will agree with the basic Mercier-style point that costs can carry evidential weight, but they may resist the near-axiomatic framing unless you also specify when “unexpected source” rationally _decreases_ credibility.

Notably, several of the transcripts you quote already motivate such boundary conditions: misattribution, context-stripping, strategic laundering, performative heterodoxy, or non-representativeness (“authenticity… representativeness,” §2). Those are not mere confounds; they are exactly the cases where “against-type” should not automatically increase credibility—and where a penalty can be epistemically warranted even if the argument’s logical form is unchanged.

This becomes especially important because §7 elevates “Costly signal crediting” to constitutional status. Without an explicit account of when costly-signaling logic applies, when it is defeated, and what a system should do when it cannot responsibly estimate “cost” or sincerity, the principle can read as a politically charged rule-of-thumb (“credit contrarianism”) rather than a defeasible governance-safe norm. My suggestion would be to write the boundary conditions right into §6–§7 as part of the positive content of the Liberal constitution (i.e., not just “credit against-interest testimony,” but “treat against-interest as potentially probative _subject to_ tests for attribution reliability / laundering risk / context loss,” etc., in the terms you are already using in §2).

## The Platonic/Liberal taxonomy as an analytic device rather than a foil

The Platonic/Liberal distinction in §§5–6 is where the manuscript makes its core normative move, and it is also where readers are most likely to query whether the taxonomy is carrying more than one separable commitment at once. As written, “Platonic” sometimes bundles: formal argument appraisal, objectivism about truth, institutional authoritarianism, “approved sources,” default source-independence, and high-confidence canonicity (§5 “The Platonic Approach”). That bundling makes it easy for a skeptical reader to treat “Platonic” as a rhetorical foil rather than a live option in the design space.

There is also a tension worth resolving explicitly: the paper at points emphasizes epistemic humility (“we do not know what correct epistemic behavior is,” Conclusion; §6), while also leaning heavily on costly-signaling / epistemic-vigilance logic as a substantive correctness constraint (§3; §6–§7). One way through is to be clearer about which parts of your proposal are (i) procedural legitimacy constraints (what must be contestable/justifiable), versus (ii) substantive epistemic theses you are willing to build into the constitution (e.g., a defeasible role for costly signaling).

If you can charitably reconstruct non-authoritarian “Platonic” options that still accommodate testimony/source reliability (so the contrast is not “Liberal or authoritarianism”), the Liberal proposal will look less like labeling and more like an illumination of genuine governance tradeoffs.

## Making the “constitutional core” contestable/auditable in §7

§7 is positioned as the centerpiece contribution—“explicit, contestable” governance via eight principles and four orientations—but several items currently read as high-level desiderata rather than constraints that could structure auditing, adjudication, and revision. This is particularly salient for the legitimacy-sensitive components: how “expected position” is determined without laundering partisan priors; how “costs of deviation” are estimated without encoding asymmetric social narratives; and how symmetry is enforced in light of your motivating evidence of asymmetric penalties (§2 “Asymmetric Penalties”).

If the constitution analogy is meant to do real work, readers will look for something like a thin “auditing interface” that connects §2’s observed behaviors to §7’s principles in an operational way: concrete failure modes, observable proxies in transcripts/ratings, and an explicit contestation/update pathway for stakeholders who disagree about the expected-position map or the cost model. Even a minimal governance template—“what is logged, what triggers review, who can challenge what, what counts as a successful revision”—would make the constitution feel like an instrument that can be contested and improved, rather than a set of virtues we hope a system instantiates.

The nice opportunity here is that you already have the raw materials: §2 gives concrete behavioral signatures (e.g., authenticity/representativeness rationales; mandate-fit boosts; “evaluate on merits alone” suppression). §7 could become more powerful by tying each constitutional element to one of those signatures and specifying what an auditor (or an affected stakeholder) would be able to point to when asserting non-compliance or pressing for revision.
