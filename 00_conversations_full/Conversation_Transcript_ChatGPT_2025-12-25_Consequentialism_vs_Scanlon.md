Consequentialism and Scanlonian contractualism differ first in what makes an act (or a principle) right.

Consequentialism: the fundamental standard is outcome value. An act/principle is right if it produces (expected) consequences that are better—often “best overall” or at least “no worse than alternatives”—according to some value function (utility, welfare, preference satisfaction, etc.).

Scanlon (contractualism): the fundamental standard is justifiability to others. An act is wrong if it would be permitted by a principle for general regulation that someone could reasonably reject, given the burdens that principle would impose on them (and the reasons others have to insist on it). The key unit isn’t “overall value,” but individual persons’ reasons, especially their complaints against being treated in certain ways.

So you’re exactly right that reasonable rejectability can be sensitive to consequences. What changes is how consequences enter the moral test: not as an impersonal maximand, but as person-grounded reasons that count for or against principles.

How consequences matter in Scanlon: broader and more flexible than “maximize good”
Here are some of the main routes—many of which are hard to represent cleanly in a single consequentialist metric, and that’s part of Scanlon’s point.

1) Consequences as burdens and benefits to identifiable standpoints
In Scanlon, consequences matter because they determine what it would be like for different people if a principle were allowed. Those effects generate reasons to reject (or support) a principle.

“This principle would expose me to serious harm/risk/humiliation” is the paradigmatic consequential input.

But it’s not “harm counted in a sum”; it’s “harm as a complaint someone can press.”

2) Consequences matter distributionally, not just in totals
A lot of the flexibility comes from the fact that Scanlon is naturally attentive to who bears what, not just the aggregate outcome.

A principle that yields a slightly better overall outcome by imposing severe burdens on a few is precisely the kind of thing that can be reasonably rejectable.

The contractualist question isn’t “Does the gain outweigh the loss?” but “Could those who are made to bear the loss reasonably object to being asked to accept this principle, given what others gain?”

This makes consequences matter in a non-aggregative way: the distribution can be morally decisive even when the sum looks good.

3) Consequences include risk exposure and expected-value structure
Scanlon is very comfortable treating risk as a morally relevant consequence—often more directly than simple act-utilitarian talk, because risk shows up as a complaint: “You’re asking me to live under a principle that predictably subjects me to unacceptable risk.”

This is broader than “did the bad thing happen?” because it captures:

ex ante risk imposition,

uncertainty,

who is made vulnerable,

whether the risk is avoidable/consensual.

4) Consequences for assurance, trust, and reliance
A distinctively Scanlonian way consequences matter is through what people can reasonably rely on in one another.

Example: a principle permitting “small lies for convenience” might have tiny immediate harms in each instance. But it can be rejectable because of a different kind of consequence:

it undermines assurance (“I can’t rely on what you say”),

it destabilizes expectations that structure cooperation,

it changes what it’s like to occupy the social role of “speaker/hearer.”

These are consequences, but they’re not well captured as just “units of welfare.” They’re consequences for the normative relations between people.

5) Consequences for the social meaning of actions and forms of respect
Scanlon treats some “consequences” as about what an action expresses or what it does to someone’s standing—humiliation, degradation, treating someone as disposable, etc.

Even if the welfare outcome were similar, principles can differ in how they position people:

“This policy treats some as instruments”

“This policy fails to recognize someone’s status as an equal”
Those are consequences for standing and recognition—again: reasons people can press in rejection.

6) Consequences at the level of practices and institutions
Contractualism evaluates principles meant for general regulation. That invites a practice-level perspective on consequences:

What happens if everyone is permitted to do this?

What incentives, norms, and equilibria does that generate?

What kinds of institutions become necessary (monitoring, enforcement, policing)?

Does it predictably erode valuable practices (promising, testimony, professional roles, democratic deliberation)?

This can look “rule-consequentialist,” but the selection criterion differs: Scanlon asks whether the principle’s practice-level consequences create rejectable burdens for some, not whether the practice maximizes the good.

7) Consequences are compared via multiple kinds of reasons, not one currency
A very general way to put the flexibility point: for Scanlon, “consequences” can matter under many descriptions—harm, risk, unfairness, betrayal, domination, loss of assurance, disrespect—because these are distinct normative reasons. You don’t have to translate them into one homogeneous stuff.

Consequentialism often wants a single ranking of outcomes; Scanlon lets the moral landscape be plural.

The key contrast with rule-consequentialism (since it’s the closest cousin)
You can say: both views can be “principle-based,” but they answer different questions.

Rule-consequentialism: choose the set of rules whose general acceptance produces the best overall results. Losers’ burdens matter insofar as they affect the overall score.

Scanlon: choose principles that no one could reasonably reject. Losers’ burdens matter as standalone grounds of rejection that may not be permissibly traded away for sufficiently many small gains to others.

So when you say “reasonable rejectability could focus on consequences,” the Scanlonian reply is: yes—but it focuses on them as they bear on interpersonal justification: what you are asking each person to live with, and whether that ask can be justified to them as an equal.

A quick example to crystallize the “broader consequence” idea
Consider a principle: “Doctors may disclose patient information when doing so produces modest public benefits.”

A consequentialist might say: if the disclosure yields enough benefit (better public health messaging, efficiency), it’s justified.

A Scanlonian is likely to spotlight a different “consequence profile”:

loss of assurance for patients,

erosion of trust needed for healthcare to function,

the particular burden on those who become vulnerable to stigma, coercion, or discrimination,

the standing-based wrong of being treated as a resource for public aims.

Those are all consequences—but they matter through complaints, assurance, standing, and practice-level stability, not through a single maximizing calculus.

What is solidly in Elgin (2008) “Trustworthiness”—and is “good enough” for your purposes—is a Scanlon-echo in form (not a full contractualist apparatus):

1) The core “unrejectability” criterion (closest thing to the Scanlonian hook)
Elgin’s definitional claim is that trustworthiness depends on being backed by reasons that other members of the relevant epistemic community “cannot reasonably reject.” This appears in the PhilPapers abstract and reflects the paper’s central proposal. 
PhilPapers

That’s the clean citeable bit: epistemic status via intersubjective (un)rejectability of reasons, rather than via maximizing epistemic goods.

2) The elaboration you found: “cognitive environment” + historical shift in rejectability
The passage you quote (public standards of evidence/relevance; what “could not reasonably be rejected” at one time later becomes rejectable; stress/ulcer example) is exactly where Elgin cashes out what “cannot reasonably reject” means epistemically: it’s indexed to a community’s background assumptions and standards, and it’s dynamic across inquiry. That language is directly visible in the accessible PDF snippet. 
Catherine Elgin
