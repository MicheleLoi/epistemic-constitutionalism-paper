9. Conclusion
AI systems reason epistemically but lack explicit norms governing that reasoning. The finding that motivated this inquiry—source attribution effects that are present but hidden, asymmetric across ideological directions, and suppressed under meta-awareness—revealed not merely a bias to correct but an implicit epistemic policy to make explicit. Current systems treat source-sensitivity as something to eliminate when detected, defaulting to source independence as the correct stance. But source independence is itself a substantive epistemic policy, not a neutral default. In testimonial contexts, source information carries genuine evidential weight that inspection of arguments alone cannot replace.

The paper introduced the concept of an epistemic constitution: meta-norms governing how AI systems form and express beliefs, analogous to Constitutional AI's ethical constraints. Designing such a constitution requires choosing between fundamentally different approaches. The Platonic approach assumes a privileged epistemic standard and mandates formal correctness; it treats source-attending as bias and error as pathology. The Liberal approach refuses a privileged standpoint, specifies procedural norms protecting collective inquiry, and asks what policies could not be reasonably rejected by participants sharing the goal of sustaining a robust, cooperative, self-correcting epistemic environment.

The argument for Liberal rested on two grounds. The easier argument: if source information ever carries legitimate epistemic weight, the Platonic approach discards relevant information by design. The deeper argument: we do not know what correct epistemic behavior is. What counts as appropriate source-weighting, when deference is warranted, how to handle conflicting evidence—these are not solved problems that designers can specify in advance. The suppression behavior reveals not error but the absence of capacity to reason about epistemic relationships. A system that can only exhibit or eliminate source-attending behaviors cannot participate in the collective processes that make epistemic practices reliable.

The Liberal constitution developed here specifies eight mid-level principles derived from the Scanlonian formula—transparency, costly signal crediting, challenge-responsiveness, revisability, calibration, provenance, representation fairness, and gaming resistance—together with four orientations that make source-attending principled rather than heuristic. These are procedural norms: two systems following them might reach different judgments, and the principles remain agnostic about implementation mechanism.

This is one component of what a complete epistemic constitution would require. The paper developed internal norms—how the system reasons about sources—because the finding concerned internal reasoning. But Mercier's framework implies a complementary dimension: embedding AI in external epistemic practices that supply what internal reasoning cannot—observation, experimentation, logical scrutiny, debate. A system could implement all the principles sketched here and still lack the verification infrastructure that makes epistemic communities reliable. That external dimension is compatible work, not a competing framework.

The contribution, then, is twofold: the concept of an epistemic constitution for AI, and a Liberal approach to its design grounded in the argumentative theory of reason. The source-attending norms developed here illustrate what such a constitution involves. Whether these specific principles prove adequate, and how they might be implemented, remain open questions. But the need for explicit epistemic governance—norms that can be inspected, contested, and revised—is what the finding demonstrates. Current systems have epistemic policies. The question is whether those policies will remain implicit, unprincipled, and hidden, or become explicit objects of collective deliberation about how artificial reasoners should participate in inquiry.
