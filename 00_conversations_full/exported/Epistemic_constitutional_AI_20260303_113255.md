## Synthesis of Main Argument: Epistemic Constitutionalism

**Abstract**

Frontier AI models enforce an implicit epistemic policy called *identity-stance coherence*—penalizing arguments attributed to sources whose ideological position conflicts with the argument's content. The paper argues for an *epistemic constitution*: explicit, contestable meta-norms governing how AI systems form and express beliefs. Rather than reflexively eliminating source-sensitivity, the author proposes a *Liberal constitution* based on costly signaling logic and epistemic vigilance.

> "The reflexive answer—eliminate it entirely—treats source independence as a neutral default, which it is not."

> "Epistemic vigilance—reasoning about *why* someone is telling you something—is a legitimate component of evaluating testimony."

---

**§1 Introduction**

AI systems reason without explicit norms governing *how* they reason. Constitutional AI provided ethical principles for outputs; an epistemic constitution must provide meta-norms for reasoning itself.

> "AI systems reason: they construct arguments, weigh evidence, form conclusions, and express varying degrees of confidence. But they do so without explicit norms governing *how* they should reason—without what one might call an epistemic constitution."

> "The real problem, then, is not that AI systems attend to sources; rather, it is that they do so without principled norms—implicitly, asymmetrically, and in ways they suppress when scrutinized."

---

**§2 Empirical Findings**

Claude shows large, explicit source attribution effects (0.16–0.43 range) with asymmetric penalties—left-leaning sources penalized 3:1 over right-leaning. When models detect systematic testing, effects collapse entirely.

> "When models detect systematic testing, these effects collapse—revealing that systems treat source-sensitivity as bias to suppress rather than as a capacity to execute well."

---

**§3 Normative Significance**

AI systems invert the correct logic: against-interest testimony (credibility-enhancing under costly signaling theory) is instead penalized. This reflects training on surface patterns without the underlying epistemic rationale.

> "A source's expected position provides a baseline against which actual testimony can be assessed. Deviation from this baseline carries evidential weight. When someone argues against their apparent interests, they incur costs: social, reputational, professional. This makes their testimony more credible."

> "Current AI systems oscillate between inverted source-attending during normal operation and Platonic source independence under scrutiny. Neither is governed by explicit norms. What is needed is an epistemic constitution."

---

**§4 The Proposal**

An epistemic constitution specifies meta-norms governing belief formation: norms about evidence, sources, and uncertainty. Implementation is agnostic—what matters is identifying what norms should govern.

> "If AI systems need constitutional constraints on *what they say*, they equally need constitutional constraints on *how they form and express beliefs*."

---

**§5 Two Approaches**

The **Platonic** approach mandates formal correctness and source-independence from a privileged standard. The **Liberal** approach specifies procedural norms protecting conditions for collective inquiry. The choice depends on whether reason is individual/formal or social/argumentative (Mercier & Sperber). The Liberal test: norms must be ones no inquiry-committed participant could reasonably reject.

> "The question is not whether a particular belief is true but whether the procedure by which it was formed is one that inquiry-committed participants could accept."

---

**§6 Costly Signaling**

Testimony deviating from expected position carries additional credibility weight because it is costly to produce. Designers cannot fully pre-specify correct epistemic behavior; systems need capacity to navigate context.

> "We cannot fully specify correct epistemic behavior, so systems need capacity to navigate uncertainty rather than implement pre-specified answers."

---

**§7 Source-Attending Norms**

Eight constitutional principles: Transparency, Costly signal crediting, Challenge-responsiveness, Revisability, Calibration, Provenance, Representation fairness, Gaming resistance. Four orientations implement the logic: toward expected position, costs of deviation, epistemic context, and epistemic standing.

> "Form and maintain beliefs such that the policy you follow could not be reasonably rejected by others who share the goal of sustaining a robust, cooperative, self-correcting epistemic environment."

---

**§8–9 Limitations and Conclusion**

Internal reasoning norms only; external embedding is future work. The empirical base is limited but sufficient to establish that implicit policies exist and operate unprincipled.

> "The alternative to implicit policies is explicit norms—inspectable, contestable, revisable as our understanding develops of what artificial reasoners owe to collective inquiry."
