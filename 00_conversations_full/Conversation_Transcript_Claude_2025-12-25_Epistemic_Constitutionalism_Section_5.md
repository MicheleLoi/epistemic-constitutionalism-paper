Two Conceptions of What Norms Are For
The distinction runs deep. It concerns not just which norms to adopt but what epistemic norms are for.
The Platonic constitution certifies. It specifies what correct reasoning looks like and provides mechanisms for ensuring conformity. Authority derives from the epistemic standard it implements; success is measured by approximation to that standard. The constitution is, in effect, a specification for an ideal reasoner.
The Liberal constitution protects. It specifies conditions for collective inquiry and provides mechanisms for contesting and revising norms. Authority derives from reasoned agreement among inquiry-committed participants; success is measured by whether conditions for productive epistemic cooperation are maintained. The constitution is, in effect, a charter for a reasoning community.
These are not merely different policy preferences but different conceptions of what we are doing when we design epistemic norms. The Platonic designer asks: what should correct reasoning look like? The Liberal designer asks: what conditions must hold for reasoning to work?
The choice shapes everything downstream. What would source-attending norms look like? On the Platonic approach, they would specify when (if ever) source information is epistemically relevant—presumably never, or only as a heuristic for tracking more fundamental features. On the Liberal approach, they would specify how to reason about sources in ways that couldn't be reasonably rejected—attending to sources when there are principled grounds for doing so. How should we respond to asymmetric source effects? The Platonic response is correction: eliminate the bias. The Liberal response is reconstruction: make the source-attending principled. What counts as improvement? For the Platonist, closer approximation to source independence. For the Liberal, better-grounded source-attending that serves collective inquiry.
