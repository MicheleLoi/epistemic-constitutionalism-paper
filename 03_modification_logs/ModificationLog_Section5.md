## MOD-013: Scanlonian Derivation

**Type:** Conceptual development  
**Trigger:** User feedback: "Scanlon appears a bit too sudden. We need to offer enough justification to show that this is not just a lucky hallucination by AI."

**Problem identified:** Original text jumped directly to Scanlon without showing why a contractualist test is appropriate for the Liberal approach.

**Source:** ChatGPT transcript showed conceptual development:
1. Negative orientation (avoid harm to inquiry, not prescribe correct beliefs)
2. Universalizability constraint (could everyone adopt this policy?)
3. Scanlonian refinement (reasonable rejectability, not consequences)

**Change:** Expanded derivation to show the moves:

> The Liberal approach begins from a negative orientation: rather than prescribing correct belief-formation, it asks which policies would undermine collective inquiry if generally adopted. This suggests a Kantian move: could everyone follow this policy without destroying the conditions that make reasoning together possible?
>
> This framing still risks a consequentialist reading: evaluate policies by their effects on the epistemic system. Scanlon's contractualism (Scanlon, 1998) offers a refinement. On Scanlon's view, the test for moral principles is not aggregate consequences but reasonable rejectability: could someone subject to the principle reasonably refuse to accept it?

**Rationale:** Shows principled route to Scanlonian test without reproducing full discovery process. Each move is justified: negative because no privileged standard; Kantian universalizability as constraint; Scanlon as refinement avoiding consequentialism. Avoids unexplained contrasts (e.g., "not Kantian in the sense of formal consistency") that introduce confusion.

## MOD-014: Scanlon Consequences and Epistemic Precedents

**Type:** Conceptual development + citation addition  
**Trigger:** User feedback via epistemic trace (Document 019): "reasonable rejectability could focus on consequences. Expand on the broader and more flexible ways in which consequences matter for Scanlon."

**Problem identified:** 
- Original framing ("not aggregate consequences but reasonable rejectability") created false dichotomy
- Scanlon doesn't reject consequences; he handles them differently—as complaints individuals can press
- Also: Scanlonian move in epistemics has precedents that should be acknowledged

**Changes:**
1. Revised Scanlon passage to show consequences matter as reasons/complaints, not single score:
   > "On Scanlon's view, consequences matter—but as reasons that different individuals can press against principles, not as a single aggregate score to maximize. Who bears the burdens matters, not just totals. Whether principles degrade standing or exclude participants matters."

2. Added epistemic precedents:
   - Elgin (2008): "cannot reasonably reject" in explicitly epistemic setting
   - Basu (2019): doxastic wronging via failures of justifiability  
   - Crawford (2021, 2025): Scanlonian contractualism applied to testimonial injustice

3. Positioned our project: differs in focus (epistemic governance of AI) but draws on same structural insight

**Rationale:** 
- Corrects false dichotomy between consequences and Scanlon
- Shows the Scanlonian move isn't unprecedented in epistemics (less original, less crazy)
- Positions the paper within existing literature while maintaining distinctiveness
