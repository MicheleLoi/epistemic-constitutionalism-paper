## Epistemic trace of this conversation (for the chapter on Epistemic Constitutionalism for AI)

### 0) Working aim that emerged
- You want a **distinctively epistemic** (not primarily moral/etico-political) framework for AI governance of inquiry: **epistemic constitutionalism**.
- You don’t want an *artificial* firewall between epistemic and moral dimensions, but you want to **keep the organizing standpoint epistemic** (and acknowledge entanglement explicitly in the paper rather than letting the project collapse into “what we owe each other morally”).

---

### 1) Core contrast established: consequentialism vs Scanlon
**Consequentialism**
- Fundamental standard: promote/maximize (expected) good outcomes.
- Consequences are the *final court of appeal* (often aggregative, metric-seeking).

**Scanlonian contractualism**
- Fundamental standard: principles must be **justifiable to others**; wrongness ties to whether a principle is **reasonably rejectable**.
- Consequences matter, but as **reasons/complaints** different people can press against principles; not (necessarily) as a single summed score.

**Key conceptual pivot for your project**
- You borrow **the structure of constraint/legitimacy** (rejectability), while trying to keep the *currency* epistemic.

---

### 2) “Consequences matter” for Scanlon in flexible ways (the template you want to port)
We articulated consequence-sensitivity in contractualism that is **not reducible to maximizing**:
- **Distribution-sensitive**: who bears epistemic burdens matters, not just totals.
- **Risk-sensitive**: ex ante exposure can ground objection.
- **Assurance/coordination-sensitive**: trust, reliance, practice-stability.
- **Standing/recognition-sensitive**: not only harm, but whether principles degrade or exclude.
- **Practice-level**: consequences of publicly adopted principles, not isolated acts.

This became your “menu” for how epistemic norms might incorporate consequences without turning into veritistic maximizing.

---

### 3) First attempt to map this to the epistemic realm
Initial framing (provisional):
- **Epistemic consequentialism**: select epistemic policies that maximize epistemic goods (truth/accuracy/knowledge/understanding, etc.).
- **Epistemic constitutionalism (your target)**: select epistemic policies for AI-mediated inquiry that are **not reasonably rejectable by participants in inquiry**, given the goal of sustaining a robust, cooperative, self-correcting epistemic environment.

Important: you explicitly want the second view **without** letting it become a moral theory of belief (Basu/Crawford direction), while still acknowledging that epistemic governance inevitably touches trust, standing, vulnerability, etc.

### 4.1) State of the art positioning: Basu, Crawford, Elgin (and what’s new here)

This subsection captures the *closest adjacent literatures* we touched, in a way that acknowledges continuity **without** collapsing your epistemic-constitutional project into theirs.

#### Rima Basu: *doxastic wronging* and the moral constraints on belief (adjacent, but morally organizing)
- **Central contribution:** Basu’s core move is to argue that *beliefs can wrong*, including cases designed to pressure the thought that wronging must track epistemic defect (e.g., the “supposedly rational racist”). Even where evidence might support a racist generalization in a non-ideal world, there can still be something morally objectionable about holding the belief. (Basu 2019)
- **Resulting dialectical upshot:** Basu presses that we need to recognize **moral norms governing belief** alongside epistemic norms; the explanatory target is moral wronging, not primarily the structure of epistemic legitimacy.
- **How this supports your chapter (without spoiling originality):**
  - Basu helps motivate why “accuracy-first” (or truth-first) governance of AI inquiry is incomplete: epistemic practices occur in non-ideal social worlds where what looks evidentially supported can still be normatively problematic.
  - But Basu’s framework is **not** what you are building: you are not offering a moral theory of wronging-by-belief. Your project’s center of gravity is **epistemic governance** (public standards, contestability, revision, resilience of inquiry), with a candid acknowledgement that these standards will often be ethically salient.

#### Lindsay Crawford: explicit Scanlonian contractualism applied to epistemic wrongs (closest Scanlon-import, but moralized)
- **Testimonial injustice (2021):** Crawford explicitly *draws on Scanlon’s moral contractualism* to analyze testimonial injustice as a **failure of interpersonal justifiability**. The speaker can *reasonably reject* principles that would permit prejudicial credibility discounting; the wrong is cashed out in terms of **mutual recognition** and standing in testimonial exchange. (Crawford 2021)
- **Wronging in believing (2025):** Crawford extends this into doxastic wronging more generally, defending a “contractualist alternative” on which beliefs wrong when they arise from inferences licensed by principles to which others have sufficiently weighty objections—emphasizing inability to **morally justify** those beliefs to others. (Crawford 2025)
- **How this supports your chapter (without spoiling originality):**
  - Crawford shows that the Scanlonian form of “reasonable rejectability” can be made precise *in epistemology-adjacent debates*.
  - But Crawford’s governing question is still fundamentally **moral** (“can I morally justify this doxastic stance to the person?”). Your project aims to remain **epistemic-first**: you want a constitutional account of which *epistemic policies for AI-mediated inquiry* are admissible under **public epistemic standards**—even while acknowledging that those standards inevitably have moral/political resonance.

#### Catherine Elgin: “cannot reasonably reject” as an *epistemic* condition on reasons (key bridge for epistemic-first use)
- **Central contribution:** Elgin argues that *trustworthiness* is an epistemic desideratum that does not reduce to justified or reliable true belief, and that its trustworthiness depends on being backed by reasons—considerations that other members of the appropriate epistemic community **cannot reasonably reject**. (Elgin 2008)
- **Why Elgin is methodologically crucial for you:** unlike Basu/Crawford, Elgin’s “reasonable rejection” talk is deployed in a way that is naturally read as **internal to epistemic practice**: what counts as a reason depends on public standards of evidence/relevance and shifts as inquiry develops (“cognitive environments”).
- **How this supports your chapter (without spoiling originality):**
  - Elgin supplies an epistemic analogue of “unrejectability” that can underwrite your constitutional framing *without* importing the whole apparatus of moral contractualism.
  - Your originality remains: you are not just offering an account of trustworthy belief; you are proposing a **constitutional framework for AI** that organizes design and governance around (i) public epistemic standards, (ii) contestability and revision pathways, and (iii) robustness of inquiry.

**Bottom line positioning:** Basu and Crawford show how belief and testimony can be morally evaluated via wronging/justifiability; Elgin provides the closest *epistemic* foothold for an unrejectability condition. Your configuration is distinctive in (a) being explicitly **constitutional/practice-level**, (b) being aimed at **AI as epistemic infrastructure**, and (c) keeping the justificatory “currency” primarily **epistemic** (even while acknowledging entanglement).

### 6) The chapter’s philosophical “position” that crystallized
You want to build epistemic constitutionalism as:

- A framework for **AI governance of inquiry** that is **epistemic-first** (aim: sustaining epistemic practices—challenge, revision, error-correction, robust cooperation).
- It uses a **constitutional** idiom: not “maximize epistemic output,” but “set public constraints on permissible epistemic policies.”
- The constraints are justified in terms of **epistemic legitimacy conditions**:
  - public standards of evidence/relevance (Elgin-like),
  - contestability and revision pathways,
  - reliability-as-embedded-in-practices (not only point accuracy),
  - protection of the epistemic commons (avoiding fragility/corrosion).

You plan to explicitly acknowledge: these norms inevitably have ethical/political resonance (standing, burden shifting), but the chapter keeps the justificatory center **inside epistemic practice**: what a community of inquiry can endorse as sustaining inquiry.

### 7) Open philosophical questions you should surface in the chapter
1) **What is “reasonable rejection” epistemically?**  
   - Moral contractualism uses moral reasons; you need an epistemic analogue: rejectability under *public epistemic standards* (evidence, relevance, error-correction, explanatory adequacy, etc.) that are themselves revisable.

2) **What is the unit of evaluation?**  
   - Individual belief? AI outputs? Institutional policy?  
   Your project trends toward **policy/practice-level principles** (constitutional constraints for AI systems embedded in inquiry).

3) **Which epistemic goods are privileged (if any)?**  
   - If you reject monistic maximization, you still need a story about **plural epistemic desiderata** (accuracy, understanding, reliability, resilience, contestability, etc.) and how constitutional constraints arbitrate tradeoffs without collapsing into a single score.

4) **How to avoid moral backsliding without denying entanglement?**  
   - You’ll likely present a “no artificial separation” disclaimer, then proceed with an epistemic criterion: *publicly defensible reasons within inquiry*.

5) **Dynamic standards / cognitive environments** (from the Elgin passage)  
   - Your constitution must be **update-tolerant**: principles should be stable enough for assurance, but flexible enough to track epistemic progress.

### 8) Concrete writing payoffs (how this trace feeds the chapter)
A plausible chapter architecture implied by this trace:

- **(i) Motivation:** AI systems increasingly function as epistemic infrastructure; governance must preserve inquiry.
- **(ii) Two failures:** (a) “maximize accuracy” (epistemic consequentialism) is insufficient for legitimacy and practice-stability; (b) fully moralized “what we owe” frameworks shift the project into ethics/politics.
- **(iii) Epistemic constitutionalism:** a set of **public, contestable, revisable constraints** on AI epistemic policies, justified by what inquiry participants cannot reasonably reject under shared epistemic standards.
- **(iv) Elgin-style support:** public standards + shifting rejectability across cognitive environments → explains why constitutional constraints must encode contestability and revision.
- **(v) Design implications for AI:** norms like provenance, error pathways, uncertainty discipline, challenge interfaces, and institutional checks appear as constitutional rights/constraints of inquiry (epistemic, even if morally resonant).
