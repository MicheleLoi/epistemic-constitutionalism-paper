## Generalizable Patterns Extracted

### Pattern 22: Derive Rather Than State Philosophical Positions

**Trigger:** Section makes philosophical claims that could be stated or derived.

**Problem:** Stated positions lack exhibited depth. Reader sees conclusions without reasoning. For authors with philosophical profiles, this fails to meet expectations.

**Solution:** Show the derivation. For theoretical distinctions, ground them in prior questions. For principles, show the conceptual moves that produce them.

**Example from Section 5:** 
- Initial draft stated Scanlonian principle
- Revision showed: Scanlon's ethics → adaptation to epistemics → shift from beliefs to policies → final formulation

**Generalization:** Philosophical sections should exhibit reasoning, not just report conclusions. The derivation IS the contribution in such sections.

**Application scope:** Any section making philosophical claims; especially sections central to paper's theoretical contribution.

### Pattern 23: Clarify Framework Distribution Across Sections

**Trigger:** A theoretical framework (author, concept, tradition) appears in multiple sections.

**Problem:** Risk of repetition or, conversely, risk of gaps if framework assumed known. Unclear what each section contributes.

**Solution:** Explicitly distinguish components of framework and assign to sections. Document in Section Summary for forward writing.

**Example from Section 5:**
- "Mercier" conflated general theory and specific mechanism
- Clarified: Section 5 uses general theory (reason as social); Section 6 uses specific mechanism (epistemic vigilance)
- Noted in Section Summary for Section 6 Guidance

**Generalization:** When a framework spans sections, decompose it. Each section should use a distinct component. Make the distribution explicit in documentation.

**Application scope:** Any paper drawing on multi-component frameworks; especially when same author/tradition appears in multiple sections.

### Pattern 24: Make Findings Do Theoretical Work

**Trigger:** Empirical finding is mentioned in theoretical section.

**Problem:** Finding can be mere illustration (decorative) or can do genuine theoretical work (constitutive). Decorative use wastes the finding's potential.

**Solution:** Frame finding so it reveals something about the theoretical framework, not just exemplifies it.

**Example from Section 5:**
- Initial: Suppression behavior "is precisely what the Platonic approach would prescribe"
- Revised: Suppression behavior "reveals an assumption: that correct reasoning is source-independent reasoning"

**Generalization:** When findings appear in theoretical sections, ask: what does this finding *reveal* about the framework? Frame accordingly.

**Application scope:** Any paper with empirical findings and theoretical framework; especially when findings appear outside the empirical section.

---

### Pattern 25: Resist Premature Dot-Connection

**Trigger:** Section ends by explaining how its content connects to the next section's argument.

**Problem:** LLMs tend to over-explain connections, pre-analyzing material that belongs in later sections. This leaks the next section's content into the current one and reduces the later section's contribution.

**Solution:** Bridge sections should signal what comes next without pre-analyzing it. "Section 6 argues X" is enough. "Section 6 argues X because Y reveals Z" is too much if Y→Z is Section 6's job.

**Example from Section 5:**
- Problematic: "The asymmetry suggests that current AI has no principled basis... It is not implementing costly signaling logic (which would produce symmetric effects)..."
- Fixed: "For such contexts, Section 6 argues that the Liberal approach is more adequate—and that the empirical finding helps show why."

**Generalization:** Let each section do its own work. Bridges should create continuity, not pre-empt arguments. If you're explaining *why* the next section's claim is true, you've gone too far.

**Application scope:** All section endings; especially transitions between characterization and argument sections.

---

### Pattern 26: Show Route to Key Principles

**Trigger:** Section introduces a principle or test from an established philosopher/tradition.

**Problem:** Dropping in a principle without context appears arbitrary—"why this test rather than another?" Reader may suspect convenient borrowing or AI hallucination.

**Solution:** Show the route that makes this principle appropriate for this problem. Not the full discovery process, but enough to see why the principle fits.

**Example from Section 5:** Scanlonian reasonable-rejectability test derived via:
1. No privileged standard → need different test
2. Negative orientation → avoid policies that undermine inquiry
3. Universalizability → could everyone adopt this?
4. Scanlon's refinement → reasonable rejectability (not consequences)

**Generalization:** Borrowed principles need justification showing they fit the problem at hand. The route should be compressed but visible.

**Application scope:** Any section adapting principles from other domains; especially cross-disciplinary applications.
