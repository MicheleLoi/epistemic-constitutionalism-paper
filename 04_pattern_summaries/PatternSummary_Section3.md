## Generalizable Patterns Extracted

### Pattern 13: Forward References as Economy Device

**Trigger:** Section must connect to concepts developed elsewhere without duplicating that development.

**Problem:** Diagnostic or transitional sections risk either (a) under-developing concepts readers need to follow the argument, or (b) stepping on later sections' theoretical work.

**Solution:** Use explicit forward references. Name the concept, indicate its relevance, and signal where full development occurs. Example: "What the AI lacks is what Mercier calls epistemic vigilance... Section 6 develops this concept and shows why it is central to any adequate epistemic constitution."

**Generalization:** Forward references create economy by allowing concepts to be invoked without being developed. They also create coherence by making the paper's architecture visible to readers. Use when a concept is necessary to the current argument but belongs to a later section's scope.

**Application scope:** All sections; especially relevant for bridging or diagnostic sections that connect empirical findings to theoretical frameworks.

### Pattern 16: Cite Ideas Where They Appear

**Trigger:** Citation appears where technical term is introduced rather than where the underlying ideas are presented.

**Problem:** Mercier was cited when naming "epistemic vigilance" but the paragraph describing costly signaling and against-interest testimony—Mercier's framework—appeared earlier without attribution.

**Solution:** Cite at first substantive use of ideas, not at first naming of technical terms. The ideas do the intellectual work; the term is just a label.

**Generalization:** Attribution follows ideas, not terminology. If you're describing someone's framework in your own words, that's where the citation belongs. Technical terms can reference the earlier citation ("what Mercier calls...").

**Application scope:** All sections; standard citation practice but worth making explicit.

### Pattern 17: Hedge Mechanisms, Not Observations

**Trigger:** Passage presents causal explanation with same confidence as empirical observation.

**Problem:** Section 3 claimed training dynamics cause the inversion, but we only observed inference-time behavior. The mechanism is plausible speculation, not evidence-based claim.

**Solution:** Distinguish observation from mechanism. Observations get direct statement; mechanisms get hedging ("One plausible account...", "How might such inversion arise?", "We cannot confirm this account from inference-time behavior alone").

**Generalization:** Match epistemic confidence to evidence type. Behavioral observations at inference-time support claims about behavior. They don't support claims about training dynamics, architectural causes, or developmental history. When speculating about mechanisms, say so.

**Application scope:** All sections; especially relevant when offering causal explanations for observed phenomena.
