## Generalizable Patterns Extracted

### Pattern 27: Inversion Over Symmetry as Diagnostic

**Trigger:** Analysis assumes symmetric application as test for principled reasoning.

**Problem:** Symmetry may not be the right test. Contexts genuinely differ; costs vary across positions. Demanding symmetry can be a Platonic simplification that doesn't track reality.

**Solution:** Identify directional predictions. The diagnostic is whether the logic is inverted (producing opposite of what principle predicts), not whether application is symmetric.

**Example from Section 6:**
- Initial: Asymmetry between left/right treatment as evidence of unprincipled reasoning
- Revised: Inversion (penalizing against-interest testimony) as the diagnostic; asymmetry could have legitimate grounds

**Generalization:** When testing for principled application, ask what direction the principle predicts, not whether outcomes are symmetric.

**Application scope:** Any analysis involving contested domains where symmetric rules may not fit varied contexts.

### Pattern 28: Parallel Construction as Argument Substitute

**Trigger:** Draft contains balanced "X does A, Y does B" or "not X but Y" formulations.

**Problem:** These read as insights but often substitute rhetorical structure for argument. They sound good without saying much.

**Solution:** Ask what the parallel construction adds beyond its components. If removing the parallelism loses nothing except rhythm, cut or rewrite.

**Examples from Section 6:**
- "The Platonic approach treats this as noise to be filtered. The Liberal approach treats it as signal to be processed." → Cut
- "They have source sensitivity without source competence." → Cut
- "not merely adequate but correct" → "correct"

**Generalization:** Parallel constructions are rhetorical intensifiers. They belong in speeches, not academic prose. Say the thing once, plainly.

**Application scope:** All prose; especially theoretical sections where the temptation to sound profound is highest.

### Pattern 29: Verbosity-Restoration Cycle

**Trigger:** Draft is too long, then after cutting becomes too sparse.

**Problem:** Aggressive cutting can remove essential content along with padding. The question is what was essential.

**Solution:** Cut aggressively first (identify what can go). Then restore selectively (identify what must return). The restored version is leaner than the original but complete.

**Example from Section 6:**
- v1: ~1,380 words (too verbose)
- v2: ~670 words (too sparse—costly signaling underdeveloped)
- v8: ~1,044 words (restored costly signaling, kept cuts elsewhere)

**Generalization:** Two-pass editing: cut first for economy, restore second for completeness. Final draft contains what survived both passes.

**Application scope:** Any section that starts long; especially when user signals both verbosity and missing content.

### Pattern 30: Locate the Structural Difference

**Trigger:** Two approaches are contrasted, but the stated difference doesn't hold up (both could do X).

**Problem:** Surface differences may not be the real differences. If both approaches could mandate the same behaviors, the contrast must be elsewhere.

**Solution:** Ask: where is the difference that one approach *cannot* accommodate? That's the structural difference.

**Example from Section 6:**
- Initial: Platonic ignores sources, Liberal attends to them
- Problem: Platonic could specify "credit costly signals"
- Resolution: Difference is how norms relate to system—specifications vs. articulable policies; training vs. capacity for reasoning

**Generalization:** When contrasting approaches, stress-test the distinction. If one side could adopt the other's content, the difference is structural, not substantive.

**Application scope:** Any theoretical contrast; especially dichotomies that seem too clean.

---

### Pattern 31: Identify the Deep Reason

**Trigger:** Multiple framings of "why X" are attempted without settling.

**Problem:** Surface reasons may not be the core reason. Without the deep reason, the argument lacks anchor.

**Solution:** Ask "why X, deep down?" The answer that makes all other reasons follow from it is the deep reason.

**Example from Section 6:**
- Attempted: Liberal accommodates source-attending; Liberal fits testimonial contexts; Liberal is procedural
- Deep reason: We don't know what correct epistemic behavior is
- Result: Everything else follows—why Platonic is limited, why capacities not rules, why participation not autonomy

**Generalization:** Keep asking "why" until you reach the claim that grounds the others. Structure the argument around that.

**Application scope:** Any argumentative section; especially when the argument feels scattered across multiple considerations.

---

### Pattern 32: "How Do We Know" as Prompt for Honesty

**Trigger:** User asks "how do we know X?" about a claim in the draft.

**Problem:** The question might mean "delete this" or might mean "handle this more carefully." Deletion may be too drastic.

**Solution:** First determine if the claim can be supported. If not supportable, either delete or reframe to acknowledge uncertainty. "How do we know" is a prompt for epistemic honesty, not necessarily omission.

**Example from Section 6:**
- Claim: "Most contexts AI encounters are testimonial"
- Response to "how do we know?": Can't know distribution of contexts
- Resolution: Keep distinction, add that we often can't determine which context applies; uncertainty itself argues for Liberal

**Generalization:** When challenged on a claim's basis, first try honest reframing before deletion. The reframing may strengthen the argument.

**Application scope:** Any empirical or scope claim; especially claims about prevalence or distribution.

---

### Pattern 33: Section Setup Must Cohere with Argument

**Trigger:** Planning next section while drafting current section.

**Problem:** Original plan for next section may be incompatible with how the argument developed. Proceeding with original plan creates incoherence.

**Solution:** Check whether the current section's argument constrains what the next section can do. Revise the next section's plan if necessary.

**Example from Section 6:**
- Section 6 argument: We can't pre-specify correct epistemic behavior
- Original Section 7 plan: List source-attending norms
- Problem: Listing rules is exactly what the argument says we can't do
- Resolution: Section 7 reframed as capacities, not rules

**Generalization:** Arguments have downstream implications. Let them constrain subsequent sections rather than ignoring tension.

**Application scope:** Any multi-section argument; especially when later sections were planned before earlier sections were written.
